Leakage correction — putting back what the filter removed

Every GRACE filter removes real signal and smears the rest across basin boundaries. A 6° disc under a 500 km Gaussian keeps about half its peak, and what it loses reappears outside its own boundary as leakage. Three corrections are available, and they differ in what you have to believe.

Iterative forward modelling

Find the mass field that, pushed through the same chain the data saw, reproduces the observation:

mk+1 = mk + Gain · (observed − F(mk)),   F = analysis → filter → synthesis
ewh = g.synthesis(-89:89, 0:359, Quantity= "ewh", kn = kn);
[m, info] = shLowLevel.leakageCorrect(ewh, -89:89, 0:359, ...
    Filter = "gauss300", Mask = basinMask, Gain = 2);

Filter must be the filter actually applied to the data ("none", "gauss<km>", "DDKn", or a W struct from readDDK / designFilter). Getting it wrong is the one way to make the result meaningless while it still looks plausible.

The mask does the heavy lifting

Confining the solution to pixels where mass can exist is the standard variant and much better conditioned: the iteration must then explain the observation with mass inside the region, so leakage outside is removed rather than redistributed. On a synthetic disc with an exact mask the correction recovers 0.9998 of the truth and leaves exactly zero outside.

The mask must cover every region that can hold mass, not only the one you are measuring. A mask drawn around the target alone tells the iteration that mass exists nowhere else, so signal from neighbouring sources is forced into the target and the answer comes out too large. Include the neighbours, then integrate the corrected field over the target.

How much this matters

Against the published GravIS Greenland basin-average series (COST-G RL02, −231.1 Gt/yr over the matching span), with all four GravIS corrections applied to the same Level-2 input:

MethodGt/yrvs published
naive grid integral, no correction−170.3−26%
forward modelling, Greenland-only mask−259.8+12%
forward modelling, union mask (+ Canadian Arctic, Iceland, Svalbard)−242.5+5%

The neighbouring regions absorb −112 Gt/yr in the union solution — mass the Greenland-only mask had nowhere to put and therefore piled onto Greenland. The union mask also converges faster. The residual +5% is consistent with what is still missing here: the S2 161-day alias is not removed, the neighbour outlines are coarse boxes, and GravIS inverts seven drainage basins jointly rather than one region.

Convergence is judged on the change of the solution, not on the residual. With a mask the problem is inconsistent — no field confined to the region can reproduce an observation that has energy outside it — so the residual settles at a nonzero floor while the solution is perfectly converged. Stopping on the residual would report failure on exactly the case the mask exists for.

Stopping is regularisation v3.5.0

This is an ill-posed inverse problem, and the iteration semiconverges: the error against the truth falls, reaches a minimum, then rises as the iteration starts fitting noise. The residual meanwhile keeps shrinking — so "iterate until nothing changes" is exactly the wrong instruction. On the reference problem the final solution is 361× worse than the best one, and a step tolerance of 1e-3 stops 89× past the optimum (1e-4 never triggers).

Pass NoiseLevel and the run stops on the discrepancy principle (Morozov): once the residual reaches the noise level of the data, fitting it more closely is fitting noise. On the reference problem that lands within 2% of the best attainable solution.

noise = rms(field(openOcean));            % > 1000 km from any coast
[m, info] = shLowLevel.leakageCorrect(obs, lat, lon, Filter = "gauss300", ...
    Mask = unionMask, NoiseLevel = noise);
info.stoppedBy      % "discrepancy" | "step" | "maxIter" - always check
Without NoiseLevel the iteration is unregularised, the answer depends on MaxIter, and a warning says so. On a noisy version of the reference disc the unregularised run took 239 iterations and was 4.3× further from the truth than the run stopped at the noise level after 2.

Where NoiseLevel comes from v3.5.1

shLowLevel.oceanRMS gives the standard estimate: the area-weighted RMS of the field over the open ocean, i.e. ocean points more than 1000 km from any coast. Far from land a GRACE field should contain almost no real signal, so what remains is error — which is why processing centres quote this number to compare filters and solutions.

noise = shLowLevel.oceanRMS(ewh, lat, lon, isOcean);   % MinDistanceKm = 1000
m = shLowLevel.leakageCorrect(ewh, lat, lon, Filter = "gauss300", ...
        Mask = unionMask, NoiseLevel = noise);

The ocean mask is user-supplied, like Love numbers: base MATLAB's coastlines data set is not present in every installation, and a wrong mask silently changes the number. The average is area-weighted by default, because an unweighted RMS over a lat/lon grid over-counts the polar rows — on white noise the two agree, which is exactly why the difference is easy to miss until the field has structure. Plot info.mask once before trusting the value.

Two traps in the discrepancy principle v3.5.2

The residual is measured where the model is responsible. With a mask, the model describes one region while the data contains every other mass signal on the globe, so a global residual never falls to the noise level and the principle silently never fires. Reproducing the GravIS Greenland series: residual 0.0085 globally against 0.0008 near the mask, noise level 0.0024. ResidualRegion defaults to the mask; info.residualRMSGlobal shows the gap, and a large one means unmodelled signal elsewhere.

The noise level must match the quantity being inverted. oceanRMS of a monthly field is its noise. Applied to a trend field it returns 0.0024 m/yr, but most of that is real barystatic sea-level rise - and using it stopped the iteration after 4 steps, 13% low. The trend's noise is the monthly noise propagated through the fit:

sigMon   = shLowLevel.oceanRMS(residGrid, lat, lon, openOcean);
sigTrend = sigMon / sqrt(sum((epochs - mean(epochs)).^2));   % 19x smaller

Gain and iteration count

Small regions under a strong filter converge slowly: the 6° disc needs about 260 iterations at Gain = 1 and about 170 at Gain = 2. Values around 2–3 accelerate safely; large gains diverge, which is detected and raised as shLowLevel:leakageCorrect:diverged rather than returned as a field that looks like a result. The field is accurate well before the step criterion is met — 0.997 of the truth after 80 of those 260 iterations — so a run that stops at MaxIter is usually still usable; check info.step.

Gridded scaling factors

Push a model series through the same chain and regress, per pixel, true on filtered:

k = shLowLevel.gridScaling(modelEWH, -89:89, 0:359, Filter = "gauss300");
corrected = k .* filteredEWH;

The factors are the least-squares gain per pixel, so they restore amplitude over the model's time behaviour rather than pointwise at any single epoch. They are invariant to the model's amplitude and depend only on its pattern — which is the method's standing caveat, not an implementation detail.

Where the model carries no signal, k is NaN. The ratio would be two numerical zeros, and returning a number there would silently multiply your data by noise. info.covered and info.onSignal make a model that does not reach your region visible instead. info.kMedian summarises the pixels the model gives mass to, not all finite pixels — pure-leakage pixels correctly get k = 0 and would otherwise dominate the median.

Data-driven correction, without any model

Both corrections above need something you have to believe: a mask, or a hydrological model. The method of deviation needs neither. Split the true field into the part inside the catchment and the rest; for any filter that leaves a constant alone the filtered catchment average splits with no approximation:

gbar_c = f_c + dF_c + l_c

— the average you wanted, the deviation integral (what the filter does to the catchment's own signal, because it is not a uniform layer), and the leakage smeared in from outside. So f_c = gbar_c − dF_c − l_c is a rearrangement, not a fit. The difficulty is that both corrections need the unfiltered field. They are estimated instead from the once- and twice-filtered fields and extrapolated one filtering step back:

[fHat, info] = shLowLevel.leakageDataDriven(ewh, lat, lon, ...
    Mask = basin, Filter = "gauss300");
[fHat, info] = ts.leakageDataDriven(lat, lon, Filter = "gauss300", ...
    kn = kn, Mask = basin);          % the same from a series (v5.6.0)

The leakage extrapolation corrects a phase difference as well as an amplitude — out-of-phase leakage is exactly the case a scale factor cannot fix. The deviation integral deliberately gets no phase correction: Vishwakarma measured those phase differences and found no consistent relation, so applying one would be invention. That asymmetry is in the method, not an oversight here.

The function wants a series, not an epoch: both steps are regressions over time. It refuses below 12 epochs, and refuses NaN, because neither regression can skip a gap — a confident phase from four months of data would be worse than no answer.

Closed-loop, it removes 55 % of the RMS damage in testCorrectness and 63 % in the Python oracle. See info.leakage and info.deviation for the two terms it removed, and info.phaseDeg for the shift it applied.

The method of scale is the thesis's other correction, and the same function computes it: Method = "scale" removes the same leakage estimate and then scales what remains, fHat = s (gbar_c − lhat), instead of subtracting a deviation integral. The factor s = ∫R / ∫R·Rbar depends on the catchment and the filter only, and info.scale reports it whichever method ran. It rests on a uniform-layer approximation: it repairs the attenuation exactly only when the signal inside the catchment is uniform, and s grows as the catchment shrinks, so errors in small catchments are amplified. The closed loops favour it (75 % in testCorrectness, 79 % in the oracle), but both put a uniform layer inside the catchment — the case it is exact for — so that ranks nothing, and the method of deviation stays the default.

One reader of the Filter vocabulary v5.7.0

Every function here that takes Filter - and the chains that call them - reads it with shLowLevel.filterSpec: "none", "gauss<radiusKm>" with a radius greater than zero, "DDK1" to "DDK8", or a W struct. Before v5.7.0 eight functions each parsed it on their own and did not agree. Measured on the old code: twsChain turned "gaussX" into NaN results without any error; the ocean chains read any text as a Gaussian, so "DDK3" became a radius of NaN, which makes nearly every weight NaN; and a negative radius gave weights of about 1 — no filter — in the four chains that never checked it. A malformed spec is now refused before any data is read, under each function's own identifier.

Validation

Both methods were implemented and checked in Python (tools/dev/validate_leakage.py) before the MATLAB port, against known truth: exact analysis/synthesis round trip (4e-16), zero in / zero out, monotone residual, the divergence bound, amplitude invariance of k to 1e-15, and error reduction on a field whose mixture differs from the model's.

Declare the filter the FORWARD MODEL sees, not the one your input file carries. Reproducing the GravIS Greenland series, the inputs are genuinely unfiltered Level-2B coefficients — and passing Filter="none" was still wrong, because the method filters the forward model identically to the observations (a Wiener filter, approximately a Gaussian of 4° latitude half-width). Declaring Filter="gauss445" instead moved the answer from −234.9 to −227.4 Gt/yr against a published −231.1: a 1.6% overshoot became a 1.6% undershoot. This single choice mattered more than the mask, the polygon resolution and the tidal alias combined.

Validated against GravIS

The full worked comparison against the published GravIS Greenland basin averages — every processing step, the code, and what each step is worth — is chapter "Validation against GravIS" of the workflow guide. Summary: matching the four GravIS Level-2B corrections, declaring the filter the forward model sees ("gauss445") and stopping on the discrepancy principle with the propagated trend noise gives −224.6 Gt/yr against a published −231.1, i.e. 2.8%; the union mask alone, stopped at a fixed iteration count, had reached −240.0 (4%). What remains is attributable to the joint seven-basin inversion GravIS uses and to coarse neighbour outlines.
